Section 01

The inbound engine: content, and the 30/30 window

Three posts a week and a disciplined commenting practice. Neither is new, both are already settled. What this page adds is the shape underneath them, the hook, bait, offer and call to action structure applied without breaking a single voice law, and the exact mechanism by which a reader moves from the feed to the page.

Why this cadence is right, on the evidence

Her settled Monday, Tuesday, Thursday rhythm is not a compromise. Richard van der Blom's 2026 analysis of 1.3 million posts across 50,000 creators finds the optimum has fallen from five or six posts a week to two to four, and that top creators succeed on three. She is already on the number.

More usefully, almost every prohibition in doc 04 is now an algorithmic advantage rather than a cost. That is a rare alignment and it is worth stating to her plainly, because it answers the belief named at the end of doc 01, that being visible online makes somebody at this level look less exclusive.

Hook, bait, offer, call to action, inside her voice laws

The four part structure is sound and we are using it. What has to change is that three of the four parts, written the ordinary way, break doc 04 outright. Here is the translation, and it is not a softening. It is the same mechanic aimed at a reader who has been sold to for thirty years.

01 · Hook

The declined obvious

Her six openers in doc 04 are the hook bank. The strongest for this audience is the declined obvious: name the expected response, refuse it, go elsewhere.

Never: a rhetorical question, a statistic, a credential, or a restatement of the thing she is responding to.

02 · Bait

The recognition

Not a promise and not a tease. The bait is the moment the reader recognises herself in a sentence nobody has said to her. Doc 02 names the right hand column as what is actually missing, and that column is the bait.

Never: a state word aimed at the reader. The condition is named in the abstract and in the third person.

03 · Offer

The permission

Doc 01 is unambiguous. The mechanism is permission. What a post gives is not a framework and not a solution. It is the sense that somebody has just made allowed the thing the reader already suspected.

Never: a price, a link in the body, or an application facing line inside the first thirty days.

04 · Call to action

The absence of one

Doc 04 bans landing on a question to the reader, an offer, a link, or a summary. The landing is under twelve words, turns back to her life, and does not resolve.

The action lives elsewhere: in the profile, which the reader visits because the post did not ask her to.

The one place this differs from the standard playbook

In most funnels the post carries the call to action. Here the post carries none, and the profile carries all of it. That is not a stylistic preference, it is structural: her three permitted placements are all on the profile, and her landing rules forbid the ask. So the post's entire job is to be good enough that somebody clicks her name. Section 02 is therefore load bearing in a way it would not be for another account.

The 30/30 window: thirty minutes in, thirty minutes out

The single most useful finding in the research is not about posting at all. Extrovert's dataset of 150,000 connection requests and 800,000 messages measures what visible prior presence is worth, and the numbers are unusually clean.

ApproachAcceptance
Cold, no prior engagement27%
After one or two comments on their content41%
After four or more comments36%
Sent within 24 hours of the last comment44%
Sent a month after the last comment33%

Three readings that change the practice

One or two beats four or more. 41 against 36. Over engagement reads as pursuit. This is the empirical version of her instinct and it should be quoted to her in those words.

Recency beats volume. 44 within a day, 33 at a month. The engagement has to be live in their memory.

Orbit works nearly as well as direct. Being visibly present in the same conversations, without ever addressing the person, produces almost the same warmth. For an audience allergic to being approached, this is the most valuable line in the whole research file.

What the window actually is, daily

Thirty minutes in

Her own post

The first sixty to ninety minutes determine roughly seventy percent of a post's eventual reach. Replies to comments inside thirty minutes are reported to produce materially more comments and views. So the window after publishing is not optional, and it is the only half hour of the day that cannot be moved.

Her replies are held under the same approval rule as everything else until O2 is granted.

Thirty minutes out

The target list

Doc 18 sets the volume and it is settled: five to seven outward comments in the half hour before her post, three to five more between replies in the half hour after. Across twelve publishing days that is roughly one hundred to one hundred and forty comments a month, at 150 to 300 characters each.

The Extrovert finding does not change that number. It sets a ceiling per person: one or two substantive comments a month to any single principal, because four or more reads as pursuit. The two rules multiply rather than compete, and what they produce together is a list requirement.

At doc 18 volume and a ceiling of one or two each, the list has to carry roughly sixty to one hundred names who actually post. Forty does not fill the window, which is why question 02 on the sheet is the binding constraint on this section.

The comment itself

Under fifteen words is not the rule here

A study of 657,722 comments found average impressions rising with length, from 131 under fifty characters to 261 at 250 plus. The honest caveat is that medians stay flat, so length is not the lever. Relevance is, and speed is: commenting inside thirty minutes of publication is the strongest identified factor.

Doc 04 bans every stock opener. That ban is also what makes her comments work.

Everything in this section is amber until O2

Doc 07 rule five is explicit: until standing green lane approval for engagement comments is granted, every comment is drafted, batched and held, and nothing goes live. The 30/30 window as described above runs at a fraction of its speed under that condition. O2 is therefore not an administrative item, it is the throttle on the entire inbound engine, and it should be the first thing asked on the next call.

Section 03

The diagnostic. The entry test that qualifies a stranger before it captures one

This is the piece that turns a reader into a name on a list she owns, and it is the only entry asset in this plan. It has three jobs and it is judged on all three. Bring new people into the funnel. Take an email address with consent. Return a score that tells us which of them is worth her time. A download does the first two and nothing else, which is why nothing in this section is a download.

The eight rules that make it an instrument rather than a quiz

Largely inferred from the successful examples rather than from published research. Flagged as such, because the literature on this is thin and anyone claiming otherwise is guessing.

1
Some answers are genuinely good news. If every path routes to you need help, she detects the rig. Value Builder's top scorers are told their businesses attract far better offers, and that single decision is what makes the low scores believable.
2
The questions are ones she cannot answer instantly. A question she has to stop and think about does more work than any copy on the page.
3
Five dimensions, scored separately, no aggregate number at first. Benchmarking against other respondents can be added after roughly 200 responses, not before. Never claim a benchmark that does not exist.
4
It asks about her, not her business. This is the differentiator from every M&A firm in Zurich, and it is precisely what PREScore proved will work.
5
Twelve to sixteen questions, six to eight minutes. Length is the credibility signal and it has a ceiling. Five questions is a quiz and reads as marketing inside three lines. Sixteen questions that ask hard things is an instrument. Past seven or eight minutes abandonment climbs and answer quality visibly degrades, which is the SurveyMonkey finding, so the range sits just under it and beside the eight minutes PREScore takes.
6
No progress gamification, no confetti, no percentage complete. The visual language of the quiz is what kills the assessment. Doc 05 governs this page as it governs every card.
7
One sentence of stated methodology. Even a line about how the scoring works moves the object from quiz to instrument.
8
Never a state word aimed at the reader. Doc 02's hard rule and doc 04 section 6 both apply to every question and every result line. The condition is named in the abstract and in the third person. The board member test runs on every screen.

Where the gate sits, and why

Three options exist and there is no published data on which converts best for a high ticket assessment. Gating before results maximises capture and is the move a sophisticated buyer resents most, because she has done fifteen minutes of work and you are holding it. Gating at the results page with a partial reveal is the defensible middle. No gate at all is highest trust and lowest capture.

Recommendation: partial reveal. The five dimension scores appear on screen immediately, unconditionally. The written interpretation, which is asset two above, is what the email address buys. Plus the UBS consent split: one box for the interpretation, a separate unticked box for her letter. Under GDPR, making delivery conditional on marketing consent risks the consent not being freely given, so unbundling is both the compliant move and the dignified one.

Two decisions only she can make

O5 applies. If the instrument needs a physical object she has not given us, we ask her rather than inventing one. Doc 04 is explicit that she produces them instantly in conversation.

The title is hers. Doc 05 requires a title that is a scene or a question, never a listicle promise. We do not name it. We bring her three of her own lines from doc 11 and doc 22 and let her choose.

Section 04

The newsletter and the list. The one asset that cannot be taken away

There is one finding in the research that has to be settled before anybody builds anything, because it is a legal exposure rather than a preference, and it is frequently misunderstood.

How this connects

Stage three of seven. The letter is where a name becomes a relationship.

Two doors reach it and no others. The diagnostic in section 03 carries a separate, unticked consent box, and a new follower on the profile is prompted by the platform itself. Both write the same tag, letter-subscribed, with the date consent was given. Nothing else is ever added to this list by hand.

One issue a month. Band A readers get the letter like everybody else, and their sequence runs beside it rather than instead of it.

Read this first

A LinkedIn newsletter subscriber list cannot be exported. It is not an email list and it must never be treated as one.

There is no native export, no API endpoint, and no inclusion in the LinkedIn data archive. You can see who subscribes, because the list is browsable as profiles. You never get an email address. A LinkedIn newsletter is a rented audience with no forwarding address, and if the newsletter is deleted or the account is ever actioned, the list is gone with no recovery.

Subscribing is consent to receive notifications on LinkedIn. It is not consent under Swiss unfair competition law, the ePrivacy Directive or GDPR to receive marketing email.

The scraper route is closed. Tools exist that extract subscriber identities and enrich them with third party email addresses. Using one breaches LinkedIn's user agreement, produces a list with no lawful basis under either the revised Swiss act or GDPR, and would be exactly the kind of move her audience finds disqualifying if it ever surfaced. Rule it out on trust grounds before ruling it out on legal grounds.

What follows from that

The newsletter is a distribution channel that points at an owned list. It is not the list. The design is that every issue carries one in body reference to the same page as every other door, and that page is where consent is actually given and recorded. It is the only door that sits inside her own published article, where a reference is ordinary editorial behaviour rather than a placement, which is the argument for it. It is also the one door doc 13 does not settle, so it is held as R6 and the first issue publishes without it unless she rules otherwise.

How subscribers are actually acquired

When somebody starts following her, LinkedIn automatically sends a notification inviting them to subscribe. That is the primary growth engine and it is automatic.

The consequence is unusually convenient for her. Follower acquisition is newsletter acquisition. The lever for growing the letter is posting three times a week, not promoting the letter. Nothing about it requires her to ask anybody for anything.

On first publication, her existing connections and followers are invited by notification. That one time blast happens at launch only, which makes the first issue the single most valuable one she will ever send.

Monthly is not a compromise

Among the fifty largest newsletters on the platform, monthly is the single most common declared cadence, ahead of weekly. It is entirely normal at the top of the platform.

It also suits her. Monthly signals that each issue is considered, which is the register a wealthy, over served audience responds to, and it matches doc 04's standard that nothing goes out for the sake of going out.

Swiss and European compliance, in practical terms

Not legal advice. Swiss counsel confirms the final consent wording and the privacy notice before the first send.

The rule most people get wrong

The binding rule on whether she may send at all is not the revised data protection act. It is the Federal Act against Unfair Competition, article 3(1)(o). The revised act governs how she handles the data. Both apply at once, plus GDPR for any recipient in the EU.

Article 3(1)(o), per the federal communications office: mass advertising by email is permitted only where recipients have explicitly given consent. The sender must be clearly named and concealing the sender is forbidden. Every message must carry an easy, free option to refuse future messages, and the sender must inform the recipient of it.

Swiss and EU rules land in the same place for her. Prior opt in, named sender, free and easy unsubscribe in every message. She does not need two programmes.

Double opt in, and the tension inside it

Not legally mandated in either jurisdiction. Recommended anyway, for three converging reasons: it is the standard evidentiary mechanism for proving consent under GDPR article 7(1), German case law effectively requires it and she will have German recipients, and Go High Level's own deliverability guidance instructs implementing it as a condition of good sending reputation.

The tension: if the interpretation document is only delivered after confirmation, consent is re-bundled with delivery, which is what unbundling was meant to avoid.

The resolution: deliver the interpretation immediately, on the results page and in the first email. Use double opt in solely to confirm the ongoing marketing consent. Log the consent text version, timestamp, IP and source against the contact.

Every marketing email footer carries

  • Her full legal practice name as registered, not a first name
  • A valid postal address
  • A contact email address
  • A one click, free, working unsubscribe, plainly visible
  • A link to the data protection declaration
  • One line explaining why they are receiving it, and when they opted in
  • The Swiss commercial register number or UID, if the practice is registered
  • A List-Unsubscribe header, for Gmail and Yahoo bulk sender compliance
Two items for counsel, not for us

A Swiss controller targeting EU data subjects may need a representative in the EU under GDPR article 27. Whether her workshop marketing constitutes targeting EU residents is a judgement call. Separately, Go High Level is a United States processor, so transfers of Swiss and EU personal data need a lawful mechanism recorded in her processing register. Both are flagged in section 12.

Do not use

The 30 / 12 / 3 percent generational survival figure

The most quoted number in the family business world, and it does not survive scrutiny. It originates in a 1987 study of 200 randomly selected Illinois manufacturers, 1924 to 1984. The original finding was that 13 percent lasted through three generations. The word was changed to to in repetition, which shifts the claim by about thirty years. Survival was defined as remaining independent under the same name, so a profitable sale or a successful merger counted as a failure.

For an advisor whose entire positioning is anti hype, quoting this would be the fastest way to be caught out by a sophisticated reader. Not using it is itself a differentiator.

Do not use

Seventy five percent of owners regret selling within twelve months

Widely circulated in the exit planning industry and usually attributed to the Exit Planning Institute. We could not trace it to any primary publication. Every source found was an advisor blog citing another advisor blog.

The verifiable and nearly as powerful alternative, if the argument is needed: 85 percent of Baby Boomer owners lack a personal post transition plan, against 55 percent lacking a business transition plan. Exit Planning Institute, 2025 generational report, 1,162 owners. That supports the same point on solid ground.
